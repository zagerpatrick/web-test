Introduction

A diverse array of eukaryotic cells store excess plasma membrane on their cell surface. Fibroblasts, amoeboid Dictyostelium, early-stage mammalian embryos, and nearly all immune cells maintain thin surface folds rich in membrane and poor in cytoplasm. These surface folds and filopodia serve a range of functions, from cellular adhesion to chemical sensing, but in my work, I am interested in how this organization of membrane encodes cell shape and mechanical state.

To study this linkage, I use neutrophils as my model system. It has previously been shown that neutrophils maintain a reservoir of excess membrane at rest and that this membrane is lost following chemical activation and cell polarization. In this work, I further investigate how the neutrophil membrane reservoir enables shape change and migration.

Results

To visualize neutrophil membrane dynamics, I used neutrophil-like HL-60 cells expressing the membrane marker HT-CAAX. To study the role of the neutrophil membrane reservoir in protrusion generation and polarization, I expressed optogenetic PI3K, which drives protrusion formation when activated with 488 nm light. The volumetric time series below shows quiescent, undifferentiated HL-60 cells. At timepoint eight the cells are stimulated with light to mimic neutrophil polarization. The images were captured on a single-objective lightsheet microscope. The shallow 30° angle of the lightsheet was used to stimulate only the top of the cell, resulting in local protrusion generation. The resultant protrusion generation is rapid, starting only seconds after light stimulation.

To quantify the dynamics of reservoir loss and protrusion growth I generated meshes of the cell surfase. The mesh timeseries are registered to the first timepoint to make visual and computational tracking of the individiual membrane folds and protusions easier. Fine membrane features are classified using the shape diameter function which differenties between think membrane folds and the cell body vis local thickness approximations. Individual membrane folds that compose the membrane reservoir are labeled in blue and lamellipodia protrusions in orange. Lamellipodia labels are first sparsely assigned via a least squares model trained on feature size and extent and propagated to the rest of the mesh using a graph-based label propagation algorithm. Lastly missing labels are filled in by me by backtracking lamellipodia labels to their source.
